When a hidden leak is an emergency

A hidden leak becomes an emergency when water is visibly spreading, a ceiling is sagging, the floor feels warm or soft, or the meter spins fast with everything closed. At that point every hour adds damage to drywall, subfloor and insulation, and the bill keeps growing.

Close the main shutoff valve at the house, then call the 24/7 line. If a ceiling is bulging with water, stay out from under it. A small hole poked at the lowest point can release trapped water in a controlled way, but only if no light fixture or wiring is nearby.

Water bill climbing or meter moving with everything off?

Most people only learn about a hidden leak from a bill that makes no sense, a musty smell, or a soft patch of floor. The leak itself is quiet, and the water is leaving through a crack, a loose joint or a failed fitting you cannot see.

That is where leak detection comes in: a set of methods that tell us where the water is going before anyone cuts anything open. The method depends on the symptom, which is why the first call is about what you see, hear and read on the meter, not about tools.

The meter test: is water moving when it should not?

This check takes a few minutes and tells you whether the leak is real. Do it before you call so you can describe what you saw.

  1. Shut everything offStop the dishwasher, washer, ice maker and irrigation. Make sure no one runs a tap or flushes a toilet during the test.
  2. Read the meterOpen the meter lid and find the small low-flow indicator or the dial. Write down the reading and note whether the indicator is turning.
  3. Wait and compareWait fifteen minutes without using any water, then read it again. Any change means water is moving through the system.
  4. Close the house mainShut the main valve inside the house and check the meter once more. If it still moves, the leak sits between the meter and the valve. If it stops, the leak is inside.

Slab leaks: warm floors, hissing and damp edges

Homes on a concrete slab hide some supply lines under the floor. When one of those lines leaks, the signs often show up away from the leak: a warm spot underfoot, a faint hiss when the house is quiet, damp carpet along a wall, or water at the foundation edge.

We test each branch line, isolate the leaking one and then listen along its path to mark the spot. From there you can choose between breaking the slab at that point or rerouting the line through the attic or walls, a choice that changes both the mess and the cost drivers.

Ceiling and wall leaks

A stain on a ceiling is where water landed, not where it started. The source can be a supply line, a drain, a shower pan, a roof penetration or a condensation problem on an air handler.

Moisture readings and a thermal scan separate a plumbing leak from the others. If a plumbing line is the cause, the repair can often be confined to a small access cut. If the roof is the source, you will want a roofer instead, and we will tell you that plainly.

Crawlspace and under-house leaks

Piping under a raised floor is easier to reach than piping in a slab, but leaks can still go unseen for months. The first signs are a musty smell, sagging insulation, rust on hangers and soft spots in the subfloor.

A plumber can pressure test each line from the crawlspace, then look for drips, staining and corrosion along the run. Wet insulation and mold risk make this a job to start soon rather than wait on.

Underground and buried line leaks

The water service line runs from the street connection to the house, and a leak on it can run for a long time without showing at the surface. Sandy ground may swallow the water, so the yard looks dry while the bill climbs.

Listening gear on the exposed ends of the line, such as the meter and the house shutoff, can place the leak within a short stretch. Once it is marked, water service line repair options include a spot repair, a section replacement, or a full new line.

Methods we use to find a leak without guessing

Each method answers a different question. We pick the ones that fit your symptoms.

  • Meter test to confirm that water is leaving the system when all fixtures are closed.
  • Pressure test of hot, cold and branch lines to see which one loses pressure.
  • Acoustic listening on pipes, floors and soil to hear water escaping under pressure.
  • Thermal imaging to spot the warm path of a leaking hot line behind a surface.
  • Moisture meter readings to map the edge of wet drywall, flooring or framing.
  • Tracer gas on lines where sound is hard to hear, such as plastic pipe.
  • Camera inspection of drain lines when the leak may be on the waste side.

Sprinkler, pool and yard leaks

Irrigation systems leak at valves, risers and underground joints, and the symptom is often a wet patch or weak spray in one zone. We isolate each zone, pressure test it and mark the failing fitting.

Pools and spas have supply and return piping that can be tested the same way, after a simple bucket test rules out ordinary evaporation. A wet spot beside the meter box is worth reporting, since it may mean a fitting at the meter connection, which can be the utility's side.

Leak detection for shops, restaurants and offices

Commercial buildings have more branches, risers and shared walls, so a leak can travel before it shows. Tell us the building type and whether tenants share the water, because that changes how we isolate each section.

What to do while you wait for a plumber

These steps limit damage while the line is open. Stop at the first one if water is moving fast.

Close the main

Turn the house shutoff clockwise until it stops. Then open one low faucet to relieve pressure.

Protect the power

If water is near outlets, a panel or appliances, stay out and turn off power only from a dry spot.

Photograph the damage

Take clear photos of stains, wet areas and the meter reading. They help with an insurance claim.

Move what you can

Lift rugs, boxes and small furniture away from the wet area, and set towels or a wet vac to slow spread.
