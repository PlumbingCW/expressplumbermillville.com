Where the heater goes and why it matters

Location drives most of the install. A basement, garage, closet or attic each brings different needs for venting, drainage and clearance. A heater in a finished space also needs a drain pan and a discharge line that leads somewhere safe if the tank leaks.

Moving a heater adds piping, venting and sometimes new electrical or gas work. If you are remodeling a utility room or finishing a basement, settle the heater location before the walls close.

Venting, drain pan and expansion tank

These three pieces are easy to overlook until the heater is already in place.

Venting

Gas units must exhaust combustion gases safely. Vent size, slope and termination follow the manufacturer's instructions and the code in force.

Drain pan and discharge line

A pan under the tank catches leaks. The relief valve discharge line must run to a safe spot, not be capped or reduced.

Expansion tank

In a closed system, an expansion tank takes up the pressure rise from heating. It needs the right air charge set for your water pressure.

New build and remodel timing

For new construction, the heater is usually set after rough plumbing and framing are in place and before the final inspection. Give us the fuel type, the planned location and the number of bathrooms and fixtures, and we can match the unit to the load. Related work such as plumbing installation is often scheduled in the same stretch.

For a remodel, ask for the install to be booked after drywall and flooring decisions so the access panel, drain and venting do not have to be reopened. A scheduled job also lets you pick a day that suits you, and same-day appointments are offered when available if plans shift.

A safety note on gas and combustion

Gas heaters need a leak-tight connection, correct venting and enough combustion air. If you smell gas at any point, do not turn switches or use a phone inside. Leave the building, then call 911 and your gas utility from outside. Do not return until you are told it is safe.

Getting ready for install day

A short prep list keeps the visit smooth and avoids delays.

  • Clear a path from the door to the heater and several feet of working space around it.
  • Note the fuel type, tank size and any model label details on the current unit.
  • Locate your main water shutoff and make sure it turns.
  • Move stored items, shelves and boxes away from the heater area.
  • Decide where you want the new unit, and tell us before the visit if that differs from now.
  • Ask about permit needs with the Millville Construction Office if the job changes fuel or location.

Pricing

What changes the price

Every job gets a clear price before work starts. These are the things that move it up or down.

Tank, hybrid and tankless units differ widely in equipment cost, and larger capacities cost more.

A new gas line, larger gas supply or new electrical circuit adds labor and materials.

A new location means new vent runs, drain routing and piping.

Expansion tanks, drain pans, valves and flexible connectors are part of a proper install.

If your job needs a permit, scheduling and inspection add steps. Ask the Millville Construction Office.

Insurance note. Installing a new heater is a planned improvement, so it is not an insured loss. If an old heater failed suddenly and caused water damage, that damage may be covered, so read your policy first.
