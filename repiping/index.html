When repiping beats patching

A single leak in a young pipe is a repair. A pipe that has leaked three times, runs brown and shows green corrosion at the joints is a different story. Each patch buys time but leaves the neighbors of the failed spot in the same condition.

The decision comes down to how many weak spots there are and how much you will spend in repairs, drywall and water damage if they keep coming. A plumber can inspect accessible pipe and give a straight view of whether repair or replacement is the better path.

Pinhole leaks, brown water, weak pressure: is it time to repipe?

These signs point to aging supply piping. Two or more together are a good reason to ask for an inspection.

  • Pinhole leaks that keep appearing in different spots along the same pipe run.
  • Brown, rusty or metallic-tasting water after the tap has been off for a while.
  • Weak flow at several fixtures even though the main valve is fully open.
  • Green or white crust at copper joints, or rust streaks on galvanized fittings.
  • Water stains on ceilings or walls that return after earlier repairs.
  • Pipes that bang or rattle, or a house that has not had its supply lines updated in decades.

Choosing pipe material

Each material has real strengths and limits. This is a general comparison, not a product recommendation.

MaterialStrengthsLimits
CopperLong track record, handles heat, widely understood by plumbersSlower to install, can pit in some water, higher material demand
PEXFlexible, runs through walls with fewer joints, resists splitting when coldMust be protected from sunlight and some chemicals
CPVCRigid, resists corrosion, easy to joinCan turn brittle with age and heat, more joints
Galvanized steelFound in older homesCorrodes inside, narrows over time, not used for new repiping

Follow manufacturer installation instructions for whichever pipe goes in. That includes support spacing, expansion room and approved fittings.

Partial repipe or the whole house

A partial repipe fits when the damage is limited to a bathroom group, a kitchen or one floor. It costs less in labor and opens fewer walls, but it leaves old pipe elsewhere.

A whole-house repipe makes sense when the original piping is old, mixed in material or failing in several spots. You do the disruption once, and the new system works as one design. Ask the plumber to show you which pipes are worn before you choose.

Inside-the-house piping vs the service line

Repiping replaces the pipes inside the building. The service line is the pipe from the street connection to the house, and replacing it is covered under water service line repair. A new house system does not help if the buried line is the cause of low pressure.

Walls, access and what the job involves

The sequence is similar in most houses, though the details change with the building.

  1. Inspect and planWe trace the existing runs, find shutoffs, and decide where access holes will go.
  2. Shut off and drainWater is shut off at the main and the lines are drained, with a plan for when fixtures return.
  3. Run new pipeNew supply lines go in through attics, crawlspaces and small wall openings, with proper support.
  4. Connect and testFixtures are reconnected, the system is pressure tested and checked for leaks.
  5. Close upPlumbing is finished first. Drywall and paint are usually a separate trade, so plan for it.

A leak search before you decide

Not every leak means a repipe. A leak detection visit can show whether the damage sits in one run or is spread across the house. That information helps you choose between a repair and a larger replacement.

Soft water, old houses and your permit questions

Millville's 2026 Consumer Confidence Report lists carbonate hardness of 29 ppm, which is soft water. That means mineral scale is a smaller factor in pipe failure here than in hard-water regions, so look at the pipe's age and material before blaming the water.

If your home predates modern copper or PEX, the original piping may be steel or another older material. The city says its water meets federal and state standards, and the Water Utility is the contact for questions about the street side.
