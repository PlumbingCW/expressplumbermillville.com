Backup Sump Pump Installation in Millville, NJ: Which Type Fits Your Home

By the Millville Express Plumbing team · Published October 23, 2026

A backup sump pump is a second pump that takes over when your main pump fails or the power goes out. The three common types are a battery-powered pump, a water-powered pump and a second electric pump in the same pit. A battery system suits most homes with a working primary pump, because it keeps pumping through a storm outage.

The right choice depends on your power, your water supply, the size of your pit and how much water you expect. Here is how each one works and what to check before anyone installs it.

Backup pump types compared

Each option solves a different failure. Match the backup to the way your main pump is most likely to let you down.

TypeHow it worksBest fitMain limit
Battery backupRuns on a deep-cycle battery when power dropsHomes where outages come with stormsBattery has a limited run time and needs replacing
Water-poweredUses pressure from the house water supply to move waterPits without space for a batteryNeeds city water pressure and drains water as it works

Many setups combine two of these. A common pairing is a primary pump with a battery backup and a high-water alarm.

What a primary pump failure usually looks like

A pump can fail from a stuck float, a clogged intake, a burned-out motor or a blocked discharge line. It can also be fine and simply have no electricity. Those are different problems, and a backup only helps with some of them.

If your primary pump is already acting up, repair it or replace it first. Our sump pump repair work covers floats, check valves and failing motors, and a backup added to a bad main pump only hides a problem that will return.

Age matters too. An old pump near the end of its life will fail at the worst time, so a backup install is a good moment to ask whether the main unit should be replaced.

Sizing the backup and the pit

Pump capacity is measured by how much water it moves against the height it must lift it, called head. A backup that is too small may run all night and still fall behind during a hard storm.

The pit has to hold both pumps with room for each float to swing freely. A pit that is too narrow causes floats to tangle, which is a common reason two pumps fail together.

Ask your installer what size pit you have, how high the discharge must lift the water, and how fast the pit fills in heavy rain. Those three numbers decide the equipment, not a catalog default.

Discharge: where the water goes matters

A sump pump pushes water out through a discharge line, and that line needs a safe place to end. Dumping it right next to the foundation sends the same water back toward the pit, and a line that freezes or clogs can stop the whole system.

A check valve in the line keeps water from flowing back into the pit when the pump shuts off. A second pump should have its own check valve and its own path into the discharge, rather than sharing a fitting in a way that lets one pump push water backward through the other.

Many homeowners ask if sump water may go into a sewer drain. That is a question for local authorities, not a guess, and we cover it in the Millville section below.

Alarms, testing and battery care

A backup pump you never test is a hope, not a plan. Put these on a calendar.

  • Pour a bucket of water slowly into the pit and watch the main pump turn on and clear it.
  • Unplug the main pump briefly to confirm the backup starts and moves water.
  • Check that floats move freely and are not resting against the pit wall or each other.
  • Look at the battery terminals for corrosion and read the charger status light each month.
  • Test the high-water alarm so you know it works and can be heard from where you sleep.
  • Inspect the discharge outlet outside for leaves, ice or debris that could block flow.
  • Replace the battery on the maker's schedule, following the manufacturer instructions.

Power outages and why storms are the test

The same storms that flood a pit often knock out power. A backup that depends on the same electric supply as the main pump fails at exactly that moment.

If outages are your concern, a battery or water-powered backup is a better match than a second electric pump.

Discharge and permit questions to ask in Millville

The city does not publish a rule on sump discharge that we can point to, and permit requirements depend on the job. The Millville Construction Office at City Hall, 12 South High Street, is open Monday to Friday, 8:30 am to 4:30 pm, and posts the state plumbing subcode form. Call before connecting a discharge line to anything other than an outdoor outlet.

The Millville Sewer Utility is the other call. Its staff handle questions about the sewer main, and you can ask whether sump water may enter the sanitary system. Do not assume yes, since stormwater in a sewer line is something many utilities limit.

When to install one: before the storm, not during

If your pit has flooded before, the pump runs all the time in wet weather, or you have a finished basement, the cost of failure is high enough to justify a backup now. Water already in a basement is hard to fight from the outside, and our related basement waterproofing work looks at the water source rather than just the pit.
