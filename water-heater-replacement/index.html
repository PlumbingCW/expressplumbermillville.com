When a leaking water heater is an emergency

A water heater holds dozens of gallons, and a failed tank can release most of that onto your floor in a short time. Water pooling near the base, running from a seam or spraying from a fitting counts as an emergency. So does any leak near a panel, furnace or finished flooring.

A slow drip at a pipe joint is less urgent, but it still deserves a prompt call because a small leak keeps wetting the same spot. After-hours calls are handled by our 24/7 emergency line, and we offer same-day appointments when they are available.

What to do while you wait

These steps limit damage and keep you safe. Do them only if you can reach the controls without stepping through standing water.

Close the cold supply

Turn the valve on the cold pipe above the heater clockwise. This stops new water from refilling the tank as it leaks.

Cut the power or gas

Switch off the breaker for an electric heater. For gas, turn the control knob to off. If you smell gas, leave the building and call 911 and your gas utility from outside.

Do not touch wet electrics

If water is reaching outlets, appliances or the panel, stay out and call for help. Water and live circuits are a shock hazard.

Protect what you can

Move boxes and stored items off the floor. Use towels or a wet vacuum at the edges, and keep a hose out of it unless you are draining on purpose.

If your heater is past the middle of its expected life, the repair has to be weighed against the age. A new part in a worn tank can fail again soon after.

Why tanks fail and what the anode rod does

Inside most tank heaters sits an anode rod, a metal rod that corrodes first so the steel tank does not. Once it is used up, the tank wall starts to rust. Anode rod replacement can extend the life of a tank that is still sound, but it cannot repair a tank that is already corroded through.

That is why the age and condition check matters before any money goes into the unit. A tank that shows rust at the fittings or dampness underneath is usually past the point where a rod will help.

No hot water at all

If the water runs cold, check the simple causes first. An electric heater may have a tripped breaker or a reset button that popped. A gas heater may have a pilot that went out. If you relight a pilot, follow the label on the unit exactly, and stop if you smell gas.

When the basics check out and the heater still produces nothing, a failed heating element, thermostat, gas valve or thermocouple is likely. A technician can test these parts, and the answer tells you whether to repair or replace.

Sizing, fuel type and what comes out

A replacement should match how your household uses hot water, not just the size of the old tank. A family that runs showers, a dishwasher and laundry close together needs more recovery than a couple that spreads usage out. An undersized tank runs out. An oversized one costs more to buy and to keep hot.

Fuel matters too. Swapping gas for gas or electric for electric is the simple path. Changing fuel can mean new gas piping, new venting or a new circuit, and each of those adds work to the job. Tell us your current setup and what you are considering when you call.

Heat pump water heaters as an option

Some homeowners ask about swapping a gas tank for a heat pump water heater. These units move heat from the surrounding air into the tank, so they need open space around them, a drain for condensate and a suitable electrical supply. They are worth pricing next to a standard replacement, since the right choice depends on your space and your usage.

Replacing it yourself versus calling a plumber

A water heater joins water, fuel or high power, venting and a pressure relief valve in one appliance. Mistakes on any of those can cause scalding, gas leaks, carbon monoxide or water damage. Follow the manufacturer instructions on any unit you touch, and treat the relief valve line as a safety part, not an accessory.

Many homeowners handle the shutoff steps and leave the swap to a licensed plumber. If you also want a tankless water heater, that is a different sizing and venting question, and it is worth a separate conversation.

Pricing

What changes the price

Every job gets a clear price before work starts. These are the things that move it up or down.

Larger tanks, high efficiency models and heat pump units cost more than a basic tank of the same capacity.

A swap of the same fuel is simpler. New gas lines, vent runs or electrical circuits add labor and materials.

A tight attic, crawl space or stair run makes the old unit harder to get out and the new one harder to get in.

Expansion tanks, drain pans, discharge lines and shutoff valves may be needed to bring the setup up to current standards.

A true emergency visit at night or on a weekend can price differently from a planned daytime swap. We state the price before work begins.

Insurance note. Homeowners policies typically cover sudden accidental water damage, such as a tank that bursts, but not the water heater itself or damage from long term wear. Read your policy or ask your agent before you file.
